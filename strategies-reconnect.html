Taking Turns

"Reconnect" is about keeping a conversation alive — and bringing it back to life when it stalls. The single most useful idea here is the difference between open and closed conversations, because it explains why some exchanges flow and others grind to a halt after one exchange.

Closed questions can be answered in a word or two — usually "yes", "no", or a single fact. They are useful for confirming something, but they tend to close the conversation down. For example: "Did you have a good weekend?" invites little more than "Yes, thanks."

Open questions invite the other person to say as much as they like. They usually begin with what, how, why, where or when, and they open the conversation up. For example: "What did you get up to at the weekend?" gives the other person room to talk — and gives you something to respond to.

The question ladder. A reliable way to build a conversation is to climb from a closed opener, to an open question, to a follow-up:

   1. Closed starter — "Did you watch the match?" (an easy yes/no footing)
   2. Open expansion — "What happened in it?" (invites detail)
   3. Follow-up — "Why was that the best bit?" (shows you are interested, and deepens it)

Follow-up questions are the secret to flow. When someone gives you an answer, a short follow-up — "Tell me more about that", "How did that go?", "Why do you think that?" — keeps the conversation moving and signals that you are genuinely listening. This is often the piece that is missing when a conversation dies after one or two turns.

Reviving a conversation that has stalled. If things have gone quiet, you do not have to force it. Try one of these:

   • Ask an open question about something they mentioned earlier — "You said you had just started climbing — how is that going?"
   • Return to a topic they clearly enjoyed (people light up when talking about their interests).
   • Make the purpose plain — "I just wanted to say hello and catch up for a few minutes" — which removes any ambiguity about what the chat is for.

Reconnecting with someone you have met before. A simple memory trick works well: when you talk to someone, store one or two things that clearly matter to them (if they smile when they mention something, that is the one). Next time you see them, ask about it — "How did your sister’s wedding go?" It shows you remembered, and it opens the conversation straight away.
